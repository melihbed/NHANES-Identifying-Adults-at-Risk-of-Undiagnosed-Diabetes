Data Science case walkthrough

Identifying adults at risk of undiagnosed diabetes

A calibrated screening model that ranks U.S. adults with no prior diabetes diagnosis by their probability of a diabetes-range HbA1c — using only data a clinic already has.

NHANES · Aug 2021 – Aug 2023 4,698-adult cohort Logistic regression · calibrated Reproducible: make all

02 · The problem

About 1 in 5 people with diabetes don't know they have it

Undiagnosed diabetes goes untreated for years, and the longer it does, the more irreversible damage it does to eyes, kidneys, nerves and arteries.

A cheap, reliable confirmatory test already exists — HbA1c. The bottleneck isn't the lab; it's deciding whom to send for it.

The data-science question: can routinely collected demographics and a tape-measure pre-select the people worth testing?

In this NHANES sample 116 adults with no diabetes diagnosis whose HbA1c was already in the diabetes range (≥ 6.5%) at the time of the survey
Population-weighted rate 2.0% of undiagnosed U.S. adults — CDC's combined HbA1c-or-glucose figure is 4.5%

03 · Question & data

One question, one public dataset

Research question

Among U.S. adults who report no previous diabetes diagnosis, can routinely available demographic and body-measurement characteristics identify individuals with a diabetes-range HbA1c measurement?

NHANES modules, linked on the respondent id SEQN

  • DEMO_L — age, sex, race/ethnicity, education, income, survey weights
  • DIQ_L — "ever told you have diabetes?" (the eligibility filter)
  • GHB_L — HbA1c / glycohemoglobin → the outcome
  • BMX_L — BMI, waist circumference

Blood-pressure, smoking and physical-activity modules are downloaded and staged for a v2 feature block.

06 · Exploratory analysis

Risk climbs with central adiposity, age and disadvantage

Undiagnosed diabetes-range rate, by group

lowerhigher prevalence
  • Age, BMI and waist all differ between outcome groups — Mann–Whitney p < 0.001
  • Waist circumference ≥ 110 cm carries a 16× higher rate than < 90 cm
  • Race/ethnicity and education are significant (χ²) but weak (Cramér's V ≈ 0.08)
  • Sex shows no association (p = 0.34) — dropped as a signal, kept as a control
  • BMI and waist circumference are Spearman ρ = 0.89 — redundant

Descriptive, unweighted, within-sample. Not causal.

07 · Feature decision

Keep waist circumference, drop BMI

The two adiposity measures are 0.89 correlated. Keeping both makes BMI's adjusted coefficient flip negative — a collinearity artefact that destroys interpretability.

Tested all three options under identical 5-fold CV:

Final feature set — 6 inputs

  • Age
  • Waist circumference
  • Income-to-poverty ratio
  • Sex control
  • Race / ethnicity
  • Education level

Waist-only matched the combined set on every metric and keeps the odds ratios readable for a clinical audience.

08 · Model selection

The linear model wins — the tree ensembles surrender to the majority class

Cross-validated PR-AUC (5×5 repeated)

No-skill PR-AUC ≈ 0.024 (the prevalence line).

  • Random Forest & HistGradientBoosting collapse to recall ≈ 0 — with 93 positives and a near-linear signal they have nothing to exploit
  • Logistic regression leads on PR-AUC, ROC-AUC and recall
  • Bonus: it hands back odds ratios a clinician can sanity-check
  • Tuning (grid over C, class weight) — surface flat within one CV standard error, so a parsimony rule sets C = 0.1, balanced

09 · Calibration & operating point

Make the scores mean something, then pick the dial

Class weighting inflates the raw probabilities. Platt scaling (5-fold) pulls them back to observed frequencies — test Brier 0.024.

The decision threshold is chosen on training out-of-fold scores — the test set is never touched for tuning.

Because the scores are calibrated, the threshold is a business dial: re-set it for a different workload without retraining.

11 · What the model learned

Direction is clinically sensible

Odds ratio vs baseline (standardised inputs)

raises the odds lowers the odds
  • Larger waist and older age are the dominant risk signals
  • Lower income-to-poverty ratio and less education raise the odds; a college degree lowers them
  • Non-Hispanic Black race/ethnicity carries higher odds — consistent with the descriptive gradients and the literature

Associations in an observational sample, not causal effects. Demographic features act partly as proxies for access to care — a body-measurement-only variant is worth evaluating for equity.

12 · Limitations

What this is not

  • Not a diagnosis. A first-stage screen; every flag needs a confirmatory lab test
  • Outcome under-counts. HbA1c-only misses fasting-glucose-only undiagnosed cases
  • Small positive class. 116 total / 23 in test — every minority-class metric has a wide interval
  • Observational & cross-sectional. No causal claims; not calibrated to any clinical population
  • Survey design used for the prevalence check, not folded into the model's standard errors
  • Low precision by design — acceptable for triage, stated plainly rather than hidden

A proof of concept with an honest error bar — not a device.
